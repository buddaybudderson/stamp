Corrections and changes

The record of what was wrong, and what changed · newest firstA Budday Budderson Production

An issued Calibration is never edited. If a number changes, a correction is issued under a new date and the superseded issue stands, so the record shows what was believed and when. Corrections are findings, and they are published like findings — here, and in the next issue.

This page is the program’s transparency, in the only form transparency actually takes. Transparency is not a value a publication can announce about itself; it is a habit that costs something, and the cost is this list. Anyone can promise to be open. What can be checked is whether, when a thing here turned out to be wrong, it was written down under a date, with the reason, where a reader who was never told could still find it. That is the only version of the word that means anything, and it is the version this page keeps.

Everything that changes is written down here. That is a house rule of every Budday Budderson Production, not a policy of this program alone: a correction is a thing that was wrong, and a change is a thing that is now different, and a reader is owed both under a date with the reason attached. A decision that gets quietly reversed is indistinguishable from one that was never made. Each row below says which kind it is.

Why the register exists

Kintsugi — the art of golden repair

There is a Japanese practice called kintsugi, golden joinery. A bowl breaks. The pieces are not thrown away, and they are not mended so cleverly that the mend cannot be found. They are rejoined with lacquer, and the seams are dusted with powdered gold — so that the break becomes the most beautiful thing about the bowl, and the first place the eye goes.

The story told about its beginning is that a shogun sent a favorite tea bowl away to be repaired and it came back stapled with ugly metal, and that the craftsmen asked to do better decided that a repair which cannot be hidden should be honored instead. Whether it happened that way is not the point, and never was. It is a tradition, older and finer than anything on this website, and this page is a tribute to it.

Because the reasoning is the reasoning of this page. A bowl that has been broken and mended is not a bowl pretending it was never broken. It is a bowl with a history, and somebody decided that the history should be visible. Concealment was cheaper, and easier, and available. It was refused.

So an issued document here is never edited. The break stays where it happened, and the mend is a new dated row beside it, in gold. The Calibration Vol 001 carries a timezone bug on its own cover and wears the Sunday finish permanently, so the off-cycle date can never stop being visible. The Log Vol 001 went out 115 minutes late, and the foot of that issue prints 17:31, 115 minutes late rather than the 15:37 it had promised. The first issue of The Bench needed nine corrections and printed all nine.

And here is the part of the practice this program takes most to heart. The mended bowl goes back into use. It is not retired to a cabinet, and it is not quietly replaced with an unbroken one. It is used for tea the next morning, seam and all.

One thing the gold does not do, and it is worth saying once: it does not make the ceramic stronger. Nobody breaks a bowl in order to have something to gild. The gates, the panels and the fingerprints all exist so that this page stays short — and this page exists because they will sometimes fail.

2026-09-09CorrectionIndependence — the objection clauseThe clause as published on 8 September ended a supporter’s funding the first time it objected to a finding, and drew no line between disputing a result and reporting an error in it. The clause now separates a correction, which is checked, is welcome from anyone, and ends nothing, from an objection, which does what it always did. Where the difference is genuinely unclear the claim is treated as a correction and the checking is published, so that an inconvenient correction cannot be quietly reclassified. 2026-09-07ChangeThe program’s nameNothing here was wrong. From launch until today the program was published as The STAMP Protocol; from today it is Protocol STAMP — two words, in that order, never run together and never camelCased. Two reasons, and they are separate rather than one dressed as the other. The first is the name itself: said aloud, “the STAMP protocol” is already an IETF standard of that exact name (RFC 8762, see Other STAMPs), and “the STAMP Protocol protocol” is how the old name kept coming out in the mouth. The second is money: the program is opening to reader support and named sponsorship for the first time, and a name a sponsor cannot be listed beside without ambiguity is a poor one to open under. The name is not an acronym and the five letters are still not expanded — it is the postmark sense, set out in the colophon. Everything already issued keeps the name it was issued with: The Calibration Vol 001, The Log Vol 001 and the first Bench post are unedited, their bytes unchanged and their printed fingerprints intact.
