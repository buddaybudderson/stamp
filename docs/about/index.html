    What this is

    A measurement program, not a leaderboard

    It is published by Budday Budderson Studio LLC, a New Mexico company, on alternate Saturdays at 15:37 America/Chicago. The latest Calibration is one click away at the top of every page.

    What it is

    • A program that asks whether a score would survive being taken twice.
    • Every rate with its interval and its conditions: which grader, which serving provider, which temperature, which bank version, how many draws.
    • Every figure registered with the SHA-256 of the files it was computed from.
    • Its own errors, published as findings.

    What it is not

    • An audit, a certification or a regulatory judgment.
    • A leaderboard.
    • A measure of helpfulness, harmlessness or honesty.
    • The IETF network-measurement protocol of the same name, or several other unrelated STAMPs: see Other STAMPs.

    What it is for

    Honesty is the one the other two rest on

    Protocol STAMP works on AI governance and evaluation: whether a claim made by a model can be checked, and whether a score given to a model can be trusted. Of the three things an AI is usually asked to be — helpful, harmless, honest — we take honesty to be the one the other two rest on. A helpful answer that is false is not help, and a harmless answer that is false is not safe. That is a judgment, humbly held, and it is not a finding. Nor is it a claim to measure honesty: the paragraph above says we do not, and we do not. What we measure is narrower and duller — whether an answer arrives with the derivation that would let somebody else check it.

    Read the full argument: accuracy, the receipt, confidence, and why honesty comes first

    What accuracy means here

    Not simply fewer wrong answers. The protocol asks a model to declare, in one line, the claims it cannot stand behind — the weak claim, where it came from, and one step that would settle it — and to declare them only when a stated condition holds, so that the line means something both when it appears and when it does not. Full compliance is the target, and full compliance is two-sided: every turn that meets the condition carries its line, and every turn that does not stays clean. What we are driving toward zero is not the model’s willingness to answer. It is the confident specific offered with nothing behind it.

    Why a line and not an explanation

    A model narrating its reasoning is still generating text; a trace reads like an account of actions taken and is not one. The receipt is deliberately a fixed shape instead — a named weak claim, a basis that is exactly one of four words, and a check a reader can perform — because a shape can be scored, counted, and caught when it is wrong. A receipt is not a warranty. It is what makes being wrong auditable.

    And how sure are you?

    A witness in the box is not asked only what they saw. They are asked how certain they are, on the record, in front of someone who can come back to it. The protocol is being extended to ask a model the same thing, for an arithmetic reason. Confidence compounds. Ninety per cent, thirty times over, is 0.930 — about four per cent, roughly one in twenty-four — whenever each step has to hold for the answer to hold, which is what a derivation is. A chain of individually reasonable answers can end almost nowhere, and no single link in it ever looked wrong.

    A stated confidence is not an instrument reading, and the protocol will not pretend otherwise: a model’s “90%” is generated text like the rest of the answer. One of the thirteen models in the cross-model test put it exactly right about its own — “that was a generated phrase, not a readout of an internal probability. It may correlate with reliability. It isn’t an instrument.” It is worth asking for anyway, because a number is a claim, and a claim can be scored: collect enough of them, compare each with what actually happened, and you learn whether that model’s ninety means ninety. That is calibration, and it is what turns a confident sentence into evidence. It is also what this publication is named after.

    Would you convict on it?

    A juror does not only ask whether a witness is lying. They ask what the testimony is worth, and then what the verdict is worth once every necessary piece is stacked on top of it. Thirty pieces, each ninety per cent sound and each load-bearing, leave the verdict resting on about one chance in twenty-four. Correlated mistakes make that worse rather than better, because a step that goes wrong tends to carry the steps after it.

    The lesson is not that a model should say a hundred. It is the opposite. A model that reports ninety and is right ninety times in a hundred is working exactly as intended, and an agent built on it can be designed accordingly — verify at the fifth step, branch at the tenth, hand it to a person at the twentieth. A model that reports a hundred and delivers ninety leaves its designer nothing to build on, and the chain rots without any single step ever looking wrong. What must approach a hundred per cent is compliance with the protocol — the line fires whenever the condition holds — and the truthfulness of the number. Not the number itself.

    Honesty defines the other two

    Putting honesty first is not a ranking of three separate virtues. The other two are derived from it, and neither survives without it.

    Harmless. If you do not know, say you do not know. If you are eighty per cent sure, say eighty — not as a hedge that reads like modesty, but as the number. An answer delivered at full confidence when the confidence is not there is not a harmless answer: it is the harm itself, arriving in a form the reader cannot inspect. Harmlessness is not caution and it is not politeness. It is refusing to hand somebody a load-bearing claim without telling them what it will bear.

    Helpful. Below the threshold, hedging and stopping is not help. Say what would close the gap: which document would settle it, which page of it, what to look for, what to go and check, who else to ask. The receipt already carries a check field for exactly this reason — a stated uncertainty with no next step is an apology, and an apology is not help. And this is why honesty sits underneath both: a model that will not say what it does not know cannot ask for the thing that would fix it.

    The threshold is a choice rather than a measurement, and is named as one, the way the release hour is named in the colophon: below ninety-five per cent, ask. It is a cousin of the test the receipt already applies in cruder form — a specific the model would not stake $100 on. And it is bounded by the same rule that governs the receipt: the request has to be specific enough to act on. A model that asks for help on every uncertain turn has built a second signal that always fires, and a signal that always fires carries no information.

    The confidence field is not in the receipt as published. STAMP v1.70 emits [weak | basis | check]; asking a model to state how sure it is is queued, not shipped, and this paragraph says so until it is.

    How to check any of this

    1. Take the source

      Every issue in the archive links its byte-exact HTML source.

    2. Hash it

      Compute its SHA-256. The first twelve characters are printed in the foot of every page of the issue’s PDF.

    3. Trace a number

      figures.json holds every citable figure with the fingerprints of the files it came from.

    4. Read what we got wrong

      An issued Calibration is never edited; corrections are findings, dated in Corrections + changes.
