    Towards Explainable Fact Checking, Read Twice

    The second worked example of the Ten House ProtocolsPublished Wednesday Oct 21, 2026

    One doctoral thesis on automated fact checking, summarized twice: first only from what is public about it, then from the thesis itself. The two are compared claim by claim. It is one thesis, so the page shows what happened here, not a rate.

    This time we could read the work. Isabelle Augenstein’s Towards Explainable Fact Checking is free to read on arXiv, so the top rung of the Protocol 2 ladder, the work itself, was open to us. We used it last. Pass 1 was written from four sources about the thesis and saved at 23:31:25 Chicago time on Tuesday Oct 20, 2026 (published here byte for byte, SHA-256 a744c2b866ac078a); the thesis was downloaded ten seconds later, and pass 2 read all 288 pages.
    What happened. Pass 1 made 10 claims, and the thesis contradicts none of them: 4 match it, 2 match but leave out something a reader would want, and 4 are records (a defense date, a citation count) that the thesis cannot confirm or refute. That is expected, because pass 1 rested on records and the author’s own abstract. What pass 1 could not do was answer the questions a reader would ask: it left 5 of them open, and the thesis answered 4. Reading also showed what pass 1 did not: the thesis says the Digital Services Act passed in December 2020, while the regulation is dated Oct 19, 2022; and in chapter 1, 8 of the 46 references to its papers in the running text name the wrong paper.
    Limits of this test. The same model, Claude Opus 5.5, wrote both passes in one session, and it may have met this thesis in training. Pass 1 itself named GDPR or the Digital Services Act as guesses from memory; pass 2 found the Digital Services Act named, and a “Right to Explanation” that the thesis cites to Goodman and Flaxman (2017). The questions in the gap table were chosen by the same writer. The file times above show the order of work, not that nothing leaked. Certainty scores (Protocol 4) are not shown. Worked example 1 computed them by hand while the scorer was unbuilt; we did not repeat that here.
    How each claim is labeled.
    RecordA fact from a catalog, an event page or an index: about the thesis, not in it.
    Author’s descriptionWhat the author says the thesis does, in its abstract or her summary article. Rung B: the issuer’s own words.
    In the thesis · pageThe thesis’s own words, or a close paraphrase, with its printed page number. Rung A.
    Not the thesisOur own reading, or a check of the thesis against another source.
    PendingA check we started and could not finish. The page says so instead of guessing.

    Pass 1: from what is public about it

    Four sources, opened on the evening of Tuesday Oct 20, 2026, Chicago time, without opening the thesis: the arXiv abstract page; the University of Copenhagen’s defense notice, read in the Internet Archive’s copy because a plain request for the live page returned HTTP 404; the author’s summary article; and the OpenAlex index record. They support these 10 claims:

    • Record 1. Presented to the University of Copenhagen Faculty of Science for the degree of Doctor Scientiarum.
    • Record 2. Defended Monday Dec 06, 2021; opponents Gurevych and Specia; chair Igel.
    • Record 3. arXiv v1 Aug 23, 2021; v2 Dec 08, 2021.
    • Record 4. Summary article in KI 36 (2022) 255-258 calls it a habilitation thesis.
    • Record 5. OpenAlex: preprint, 2021, cited 5 times.
    • Author’s description 6. Covers check-worthiness, stance detection, veracity prediction.
    • Author’s description 7. Contributions extend to learning with limited labeled data.
    • Author’s description 8. Presents some first solutions for explainable fact checking.
    • Author’s description 9. Three verticals: subtask, low-label methods, explainability.
    • Author’s description 10. Motivation includes EU legislation requiring explanations and platform transparency.

    Pass 1 added one caveat of its own: every claim about content is the author describing her own work, so nothing in it shows how well the thesis delivers. And it listed what it could not say, which a writer would be tempted to fill in: how many papers the thesis holds, any dataset or number, which explanation methods it proposes, which EU laws it means, and what the examiners thought.

    Pass 2: from the thesis itself

    • In the thesis · pp. 2-3 It works on the content-based pipeline: detecting check-worthy claims, retrieving and ranking evidence, stance detection, and veracity prediction. Bot detection is “beyond the scope of this thesis”.
    • In the thesis · pp. 10-11 Its ten papers are arranged along three axes: the fact-checking sub-task, the method for limited labeled data, and the explanation method.
    • In the thesis · p. 12 On check-worthy claims, a unified approach “is achievable for 2 out of 3 tasks”.
    • In the thesis · pp. 19-20 Its dataset MultiFC holds 34,918 claims from 26 English fact-checking websites, and the best model reaches a Macro F1 of 49.2%.
    • In the thesis · p. 21 On claims generated to fool a fact-checking model, that model “performs 23.8% worse” than on the original FEVER claims.
    • In the thesis · p. 22 Its explanation paper is presented as “the first study on generating veracity explanations”.
    • In the thesis · p. 34 Its explainability results are “some of the very first findings on this topic, published only in 2020”. Of the explanation paper’s dataset, with about 10,000 training examples, it says “it is very difficult to beat simple baselines”.
    • In the thesis · p. 35 The EU laws it means are the Digital Services Act and a “Right to Explanation”, citing Goodman and Flaxman (2017).

    What reading showed that pass 1 did not

    Not the thesis Eight references name the wrong paper. The thesis lists its ten papers on pp. vi-vii. A script found every “Paper” or “Papers” followed by a number in chapter 1: 65 mentions. 19 of them are the section headings and the figure and table captions that label each paper, and all of those agree with the list. We read each of the other 46, the references in the running text, against the list: 37 are right, 1 is unclear, and 8 point to the wrong paper. Seven of the 8 use the chapter number in place of the paper number; one names a different paper. These are slips in a long document, and the list itself and the section headings are consistent. They matter for anyone who cites a paper by the number in the prose.

    Not the thesis A legal date, checked. The thesis says the Digital Services Act was “passed in December 2020” (p. 35). The regulation itself is dated Oct 19, 2022: Regulation (EU) 2022/2065, published in the Official Journal (L 277) on Oct 27, 2022, after the European Parliament’s position of Jul 05, 2022 and the Council’s decision of Oct 04, 2022. So when the thesis was dated, in “January 2021”, the act had not passed. Its bibliography cites a European Commission page dated “Dec. 2020” and titled “Shaping Europe’s digital future: The Digital Services Act package” (p. 219), which suggests the sentence means the Commission’s proposal. Anyone citing the thesis for when the act became law should use 2022. EUR-Lex would not load for our tools, so Lawrence Ho saved the regulation from EUR-Lex in his own browser on Oct 21, 2026.

    Caught before publication

    In the first draft, the drafting model typed the number of matching claims as 5 when the list holds 4, and counted four wrong cross-references where there are 8; the first was caught by recounting the list, the second by the fact-check and hostile reviews of this page, which also found that our first search missed the plural “Papers”. The page now computes every count from its data files when it is built, and the build stops if they do not add up. That check catches arithmetic, not a wrong entry in the data.
