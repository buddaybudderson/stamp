    Not Quite the Diplomat, Sourced

    A worked example of the Ten House ProtocolsPublished Monday Oct 12, 2026

    How the protocols handle a book we could not read: what we opened, what we refused to say, how sure we are of each claim, which parts are in the book and which are only about it, and the six mistakes we found in our own first draft.

    We did not read the book. This summary rests on the publisher’s page, five reviews in named outlets and one interview with the author, all opened again on Oct 12, 2026 for this version, saved with a hash, and linked below. Every claim names its source and its rung on the Protocol 2 ladder: B is the issuer’s own words (the publisher, the author), C is records and reviews by others. Nothing here comes from a model’s memory (rung D). Where sources disagree, we say so instead of choosing. The four summary sections run about 2,100 words, short of the 2,500 requested: that is as far as the opened sources go without padding or memory, which Protocols 2 and 6 rule out.
    What changed in version 2. Every source was opened again and every sentence checked against it. Six fixes: two review dates were wrong (the Observer and the Independent, both read from a US-time stamp); a paraphrase of Martin Jacques sat inside quotation marks; a year (1992, for Black Wednesday) and a description of John Bolton came from memory, not from the source; and one sentence gave Patten a view that was the reviewer’s. Two open questions were settled by sources not opened before: the European Commission’s archive dates Patten’s term, and a catalogue record supports the 323-page count. The scores are recomputed under R9. A new section lists the author’s offices. The full list of changes is at the end.
    Which part is in the book, and which is only about it. We have not read the book, so nothing here is checked against its pages. Every claim below now carries a label:
    In the book · words quoted by a reviewerA reviewer prints the book’s own words, and our sentence rests on that passage. The closest we can get to the text. We paraphrase it here; the review has the words.
    In the book · as a reviewer describes itA reviewer’s account of what the book says, in the reviewer’s words. The book may put it differently.
    Chapter title confirmed by a library recordThe book’s structure, from a library catalogue.
    Not the book · …True about the book or its author, but not something the book says: the publisher’s description, a reviewer’s judgment, Patten’s interviews, facts about him, and our own summaries.
    UnclearThe source does not let us tell.
    Count of labels on this page: 11 quoted, 18 described, 34 not the book, 1 unclear. A claim can carry more than one label, because it can rest on more than one source. Claims marked “US edition” come from a review of Cousins and Strangers, which the Library of Congress records as a revised edition, so they may not hold for the British book.

    What we tried, to read any of the book itself

    • Scanned copies. The Internet Archive holds scans of the 2005, 2006 and US editions. They are lent to signed-in readers only, and their text files refused us (HTTP 401). We did not try to get past that. A person can borrow a copy with their own account and check a passage against the page.
    • The publisher’s sample. A search listed a “Read an Excerpt” section on Barnes & Noble’s page for the US edition, credited to its publisher. The page we fetched showed no excerpt, so we have not seen it, and nothing on this page rests on it. It is sent to a person to open.
    • Google Books. Its service refused our requests that day (HTTP 429, too many requests), so we could not check for a preview. To try again.
    • Passages quoted by others. The four reviews above print short passages from the book. Those are the closest we have to its text, and the claims that rest on them are marked In the book · words quoted by a reviewer. The quotations in the Foreign Affairs review are from the revised US edition.

    Executive Summary

    In one line: The whole in brief: what the book is, who wrote it, and the arguments every source agrees it makes.

    Chris Patten wrote Not Quite the Diplomat after five years in Brussels as the European Union’s commissioner for external relations, in the Commission led by Romano Prodi.ONot the book · a fact about the author, from Black Penguin presents the book as an account of what has happened to Britain, Europe and the world since 1997, written by someone close to events and newly freed from a diplomat’s restraint.PBNot the book · the publisher’s description The publisher frames it around three questions: who the British think they are and want to be, how far Europe can expand and whether the European project is a mistake or Britain’s future, and whether a fractured Western alliance still has the time and will to shape the world before India and China rise.PBNot the book · the publisher’s description

    The reviewers agree on what the book is about. Martin Jacques, in the Guardian, reads it as the second part of Patten’s memoirs, following East and West, his book about governing Hong Kong, even though Patten avoids calling it a memoir. In Jacques’s reading, the recurring theme is the shift in American foreign policy under George W. Bush and what it means for Europe and the world.GCIn the book · as Jacques describes it Stanley Hoffmann, reviewing the US edition in Foreign Affairs, describes a blend of autobiography, political analysis and policy advice, full of anecdotes and portraits of public figures.FCIn the book · as Hoffmann (US edition) describes it Ian Black, in the Observer, calls it a vivid inside picture of how the EU works at the top and the best book about the EU written in English in many years.OCNot the book · Black’s judgment

    Three arguments run through every source we opened:

    1. Britain belongs in Europe. The EU is a group of nation-states working together, not a superstate, and Britain should commit to it fully.S OIn the book · as Black describes itNot the book · Patten’s interview
    2. America’s unilateral turn damages everyone, including America. Patten condemns the neoconservative direction of US policy and the invasion of Iraq, and he argues that Tony Blair’s support for the war hurt Britain’s standing in Europe and weakened the relationship it was meant to protect.G R F SIn the book · words quoted by JacquesIn the book · words quoted by Hoffmann (US edition)In the book · as Lever describes itNot the book · Patten’s interview
    3. The rise of China and India is an opportunity, if the West draws them into solving global problems. Hoffmann adds that Patten also warns about revolts by people who feel alienated or dispossessed.F SIn the book · as Hoffmann (US edition) describes itIn the book · words quoted by Hoffmann (US edition)Not the book · Patten’s interview

    The reviewers disagree more about quality than about content. Jacques called the Europe section “the most tedious section of the book” and faulted the book for leaving out empire, colonialism and race.GNot the book · Jacques’s judgment Denis MacShane, then a Labour member of Parliament and a former minister for Europe, listed British policy failures of the 1980s and 1990s that the book does not discuss.INot the book · MacShane’s judgment Sir Paul Lever, writing for the Royal United Services Institute, praised the cool tone but doubted that Patten’s call for a return to consensus-based American leadership was realistic.RNot the book · Lever’s judgment

    Our readingThe sources agree closely on the book’s arguments and differ mainly in judgment. That pattern makes the summary of the arguments fairly safe and any statement about the book’s quality a matter of whose review you trust. We report the judgments and attribute each one.

    Experiences

    In one line: What the author lived through or saw first-hand that the book draws on, as the sources report it.

    The reviews describe several experiences that the book draws on. Each is reported here as the reviewer reported it.

    An American education

    According to Jacques, Patten opens with his own history with the United States. His father was a publisher of early American pop singers. After Oxford, Patten went to the United States on a well-funded scholarship and later worked on John Lindsay’s campaign for mayor of New York. Jacques argues that this story is typical of Britain’s political class and helps explain its affinity for America.GCIn the book · as Jacques describes itNot the book · Jacques’s own argument MacShane dates Patten’s first trip to America to 1965, after his student days at Balliol College.ICUnclear · the review does not say whether the book gives this date

    Five years in Brussels

    Black writes that Patten joined the Prodi Commission with firm views about globalization. Nothing he saw there persuaded him that a single country called Europe or a European superstate was taking shape. Black says the book offers entertaining indiscretions about those five years. The French, and President Jacques Chirac in particular, come in for criticism; Black adds that the French blocked Patten from succeeding Prodi as Commission president. The book also criticizes Tony Blair’s convictions and American neoconservatives.OCIn the book · as Black describes itNot the book · Black’s own fact: the French veto MacShane notes that Patten is kind to fellow Conservatives, rude about Labour ministers, and pulls few punches with Dick Cheney, Jacques Chirac or Ariel Sharon.ICIn the book · as MacShane describes it

    Patten describes his diplomacy with Javier Solana, according to Black; Lever identifies Solana as the Council’s High Representative for foreign and security policy.RIn the book · as Black describes itNot the book · Lever’s description of Solana The book points to progress in the Balkans, where the prospect of EU membership pulled countries toward reform, and to European relations with Russia that it calls too deferential, and it faults Europe’s record in defense and security.OCIn the book · as Black describes itIn the book · words quoted by Black MacShane goes further and credits Patten and Solana with keeping Macedonia from pulling the Balkans into a new war in 2001 and with persistent work for an Israeli-Palestinian peace. That credit is MacShane’s own assessment, not a description of the book.ICNot the book · MacShane’s own assessment

    Human rights and trade

    MacShane reports that Patten tried to make human rights a condition of EU agreements with other countries, and that Patten admits the language rarely changed anyone’s behavior. Patten is appalled that European leaders would not raise Russia’s conduct in Chechnya, and he argues that the Arab world needs more open trade, more investment and better access to European markets.ICIn the book · as MacShane describes it

    An exchange with John Bolton

    Black describes the book’s account of a conversation in which Patten urged John Bolton, whom Black identifies as the US ambassador to the United Nations, to use both incentives and pressure with Iran. As Black reports it, Bolton replied, “We don’t do carrots.” Black suggests that the exchange captures much of the book’s view of Washington at the time.OCIn the book · words quoted by Black

    On the road with the book

    Greg Lowe interviewed Patten by phone from Heathrow Airport while Patten was setting out on a publicity tour of Singapore and Hong Kong. Lowe’s framing has slips (it calls Hong Kong an island and places the European Parliament in Brussels), so we rely on the interview only for Patten’s own quoted words. The interview first ran in IHT ThaiDay in November 2005, according to the page that reposted it.SBNot the book · Patten’s interview and its repost

    Explanations

    In one line: The book’s arguments: why, in the author’s view, things happened as they did, and what should be done.

    This section summarizes the arguments the sources attribute to the book, grouped by subject.

    Why Britain struggles with Europe

    Jacques reports that Patten explains Britain’s uneasy relationship with Europe through national identity: a failure to face postwar decline, the reassurance of having won the Second World War, and an old habit of looking down on the continent. Patten urges Britain not to let its history trap it in self-regard.GCIn the book · words quoted by Jacques Black adds that Patten sees the nation-state as the basis of most people’s identity, but argues that nations must do more together on problems too big for any one of them. Black notes, in his own words, how much the single market owes to Margaret Thatcher.OCIn the book · as Black describes itNot the book · Black’s own remark on Thatcher In the interview, Patten describes the EU as a collection of nation-states, not a superstate, and says there is no European policy unless Britain, Germany and France work well together.SBNot the book · Patten’s interview

    What kind of European he is

    Lever calls Patten a Euro-realist rather than a Europhile. Patten opposes moving more power to Brussels and accepts that no single European public exists. He argues that the Commission is no more corrupt or inefficient than a British government department, defends opening membership talks with Turkey, and criticizes European leaders who ignore public opinion. Lever adds that Patten sidesteps hard questions about the European Parliament, social policy and the size of the Commission.RCIn the book · as Lever describes itNot the book · Lever’s labels and objections Hoffmann says Patten makes a strong case for including Turkey and for a union that is neither a superpower rival to the United States nor a junior partner to it.FCIn the book · as Hoffmann (US edition) describes it

    How the Conservatives lost their way on Europe

    Lever singles out the chapter on national sovereignty and the Conservative Party as one of the book’s strongest. As Lever summarizes it, the Maastricht Treaty was not especially divisive inside the party at first; Black Wednesday, Britain’s forced exit from the Exchange Rate Mechanism, turned the party toward Euroscepticism, which Patten treats as a damaging illusion.RCIn the book · as Lever describes itIn the book · words quoted by LeverChapter title confirmed by a library record

    America after the Cold War

    Lever describes Patten as an instinctive Atlanticist who is appalled by the neoconservative turn in American policy. Patten considers the invasion of Iraq misguided, criticizes Guantanamo Bay and Abu Ghraib, and is harder on Vice President Cheney than on President Bush.RCIn the book · as Lever describes it Jacques calls Patten merciless about the neoconservatives and a fierce critic of the war and of Britain’s part in it. Jacques also reports Patten’s warning that the gap between Europe and America may widen or persist, and that past unity across the Atlantic may come to look like a product of the Cold War.GCIn the book · words quoted by Jacques Patten argues that Americans are not imperialists by nature. Jacques disagrees and cites the historian Niall Ferguson, who argues that America has been expansionist from the start.GIn the book · words quoted by JacquesNot the book · Jacques’s rebuttal

    On solutions, Hoffmann reports that Patten wants Washington to return to the kind of leadership it showed after the Second World War, and to engage far more on the Israeli-Palestinian conflict, on environmental and development issues, and on reform of the United Nations.FCIn the book · as Hoffmann (US edition) describes it In the interview, Patten argues that the best stance toward America lies roughly halfway between the positions of Paris and London.SBNot the book · Patten’s interview

    The special relationship

    Patten tells Lowe that Blair’s version of the special relationship won Britain little influence in Washington and gave the Bush administration license to do as it pleased. A good ally, he argues, is not the same as a vassal.SBNot the book · Patten’s interview Black reports the book’s view that Britain, in the Iraq war, served mainly as a multilateral badge for American policy, and that in the Middle East Blair’s role became finding excuses for American inaction.OCIn the book · words quoted by Black

    China, India and the open economy

    Hoffmann writes that Patten sees the economic rise of China and India as an opportunity for the West.FCIn the book · as Hoffmann (US edition) describes it In the interview, Patten says the West must recognize how much these countries have changed and bring them into global problem-solving and governance. He calls the Doha round of trade talks especially important for Asia, wants America and Europe to resist protectionism together, and names European farm protection and subsidies as a central problem.SBNot the book · Patten’s interview Jacques reports Patten’s observation that China, like other authoritarian states in Asia, shows that an economy can grow without democracy. Jacques questions why that should surprise anyone, given how most industrial countries developed.GCIn the book · words quoted by JacquesNot the book · Jacques’s rebuttal

    Extractions

    In one line: What a reader can take from the book, and what its critics say it leaves out.

    What a reader can take from the book, according to the sources

    • An insider’s account of how EU foreign policy was made during the Prodi Commission, with portraits of the people involved.O F EUNot the book · our summary of the reviewers’ accounts
    • A Conservative and lifelong friend of America arguing against the Iraq war and the unilateral turn in US policy, from inside the Atlantic tradition rather than outside it.G RNot the book · our summary of the reviewers’ accounts
    • A case for Britain in Europe built on national interest rather than enthusiasm for European integration.R SNot the book · our summary of the reviewers’ accounts
    • An early argument, made in 2005, for drawing China and India into global governance instead of treating their rise as a threat.F SNot the book · our summary of the reviewers’ accounts

    What the critics said it lacks

    • History before 1900, empire and race. Jacques argues that modern Britain cannot be understood without the legacy of colonialism, and that the book largely leaves it out.GNot the book · Jacques’s judgment of what is missing
    • Tone. Jacques finds the Europe sections schoolmasterly at times, though leavened by wit.GNot the book · Jacques’s judgment
    • Britain’s own failures. MacShane lists what the book does not discuss: British support for apartheid, the use of overseas aid to boost arms sales, interference in the 1992 American election, and the rise of Euroscepticism under John Major. He also faults the Conservative government’s record in the Balkans in the 1990s.INot the book · MacShane’s judgment of what is missing
    • Realism about America. Lever doubts that a return to consensus-based American leadership is realistic.RNot the book · Lever’s judgment MacShane argues that the task is to make Europe work, not to lament America.INot the book · MacShane’s judgment
    • Where Patten argued. Black wonders whether Patten would have done more for Europe by arguing its case in Britain than by working for it in Brussels.ONot the book · Black’s judgment

    Even the critics praised the writing. MacShane calls the book elegant, warm, clever and readable; Jacques calls Patten a persuasive and intelligent commentator; Hoffmann admires his wit and range.I G FNot the book · the reviewers’ judgments

    Our readingThe book’s questions from 2005 (Britain’s place in Europe, the reliability of American leadership, and how to deal with a rising China) have not gone away. Readers comparing the book with later events should check what Patten actually wrote, which this summary cannot show them.
